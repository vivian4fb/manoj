01

Scalar or vector?

1.4.1 — understand the difference between scalar and vector quantities and give examples

A scalar is completely stated by a number and a unit. A vector is not stated at all until you give a direction as well — and that direction changes the arithmetic. Two 5 N forces can give a resultant of 10 N, of 0 N, or of anything in between.

  • Scalars: distance, speed, mass, time, energy, work, power, temperature, density, pressure, potential difference.
  • Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum, electric field strength, gravitational field strength.
  • Each vector has a scalar partner that throws the direction away: displacement → distance, velocity → speed.

Common error. Calling speed a vector. A car driving once round a 400 m track has travelled a distance of 400 m but its displacement is zero — it finished where it started. Its average speed is not zero; its average velocity is.

Worked example [2]

State whether each quantity is a scalar or a vector: (a) work done, (b) momentum, (c) temperature, (d) weight.

(a) work done — scalar

(b) momentum — vector (it is mass × velocity)

(c) temperature — scalar

(d) weight — vector (a force, acting downwards)

The give-away for (b) and (d): each is built from another vector. Multiplying a vector by a scalar leaves a vector.

02

Drawing a vector

1.4 — the scale-drawing convention behind every vector question

On paper a vector is an arrow. Its length is the magnitude, read against a scale you must state; its arrowhead is the direction, measured from a reference you must also state. An angle given with no reference earns nothing.

  • Write the vector as F⃗, F or F underlined; its magnitude alone as |F⃗| or plain F.
  • State the scale on any scale drawing: 1 cm ≡ 2.0 N.
  • State the direction one of three ways: an angle to a named line (35° above the horizontal), a compass form (N 30° E), or a three-figure bearing (072°).

Common error. Writing “the resultant is 8.0 N” and stopping. Half the marks in a resultant question are for the direction.

03

Adding vectors tip to tail

1.4.2 — add and subtract coplanar vectors

Slide the second vector, without turning it, until its tail sits on the tip of the first. The resultant is the single arrow from the first tail to the last tip. The order makes no difference — draw it both ways and you have the parallelogram.

Worked example [3]

A walker goes 40 m due east, then 30 m due north. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant displacement.

R = √(40² + 30²) = √2500 = 50 m

θ = tan⁻¹(30 / 40) = 36.9° east of north… careful — measure from the stated reference:

θ = tan⁻¹(30 / 40) = 36.9° north of east, so the bearing is 090° − 36.9° = 053°

Note the distance walked is 70 m but the displacement is 50 m. Different quantities, different answers.

04

Two vectors at right angles

1.4.2 — the case the exam asks for most often

When the two vectors are perpendicular the triangle is right-angled, so you need no drawing instruments at all: Pythagoras gives the magnitude and the inverse tangent gives the direction.

  • R = √(Fx² + Fy²)
  • θ = tan⁻¹(Fy / Fx), measured from the Fx direction.
  • Learn to recognise 3–4–5 and 5–12–13: examiners use them so the arithmetic is not the point.

Common error. Quoting the angle without saying what it is measured from. “53°” is not an answer; “53° above the horizontal”, or “bearing 037°”, is.

Worked example [4]

A ball is kicked with a horizontal velocity component of 12 m s⁻¹ and a vertical component of 9.0 m s⁻¹. Find its initial velocity.

v = √(12² + 9.0²) = √225 = 15 m s⁻¹

θ = tan⁻¹(9.0 / 12) = 37° above the horizontal

Quote both parts, and to the same number of significant figures as the data (2 s.f. here).

05

When they are not at right angles

1.4.2 — by scale drawing, or by the cosine and sine rules

The triangle is the same; only the trigonometry is harder. Cambridge accepts either route — a careful scale drawing with a stated scale, or the cosine rule followed by the sine rule.

  • R² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, where C is the interior angle of the tip-to-tail triangle.
  • If the angle between the two vectors drawn from a point is φ, then C = 180° − φ.
  • Then sin α / b = sin C / R for the direction.
  • A resultant of two vectors can never be larger than their sum, nor smaller than their difference.

Common error. Feeding the 50° between the arrows straight into the cosine rule. The triangle's interior angle is 130°, and cos 130° is negative — which is exactly why the resultant comes out larger than either force.

Worked example [5]

Two forces, 5.0 N and 7.0 N, act at a point with 50° between them. Find the resultant.

C = 180° − 50° = 130°

R² = 5.0² + 7.0² − 2(5.0)(7.0) cos 130° = 25 + 49 + 45.0 = 119.0

R = 10.9 N

sin α / 7.0 = sin 130° / 10.9 → sin α = 0.492

α = 29.4° from the 5.0 N force

06

Subtracting: reverse, then add

1.4.2 — add and subtract coplanar vectors

There is no separate rule for subtraction. To find a − b, reverse b to get −b — same length, opposite arrowhead — and then add it tip to tail exactly as before.

  • a − b = a + (−b).
  • Reversing a vector changes only its direction, never its magnitude.
  • Order matters here: b − a is the same length as a − b but points the opposite way.
07

Change in velocity at a bounce

1.4.2 applied — and the setup behind most momentum questions (3.3)

Δv = vfinal − vinitial is a vector subtraction, and a ball bouncing straight back off a wall is where most marks are lost. Its speed has not changed at all; its velocity has changed by twice the speed.

  • Choose a positive direction and state it. Everything after that is signs.
  • Straight-back bounce at unchanged speed: |Δv| = 2v and |Δp| = 2mv.
  • Striking a wall at an angle: only the component perpendicular to the wall reverses; the parallel component is unchanged.

Common error. Writing Δv = 5.0 − 5.0 = 0. The two velocities are in opposite directions, so one of them is negative: (−5.0) − (+5.0) = −10 m s⁻¹.

Worked example [3]

A ball of mass 0.20 kg hits a wall horizontally at 5.0 m s⁻¹ and rebounds along the same line at 5.0 m s⁻¹. Find the magnitude of the change in momentum.

Take “towards the wall” as positive.

Δv = (−5.0) − (+5.0) = −10 m s⁻¹

Δp = m Δv = 0.20 × (−10) = −2.0 kg m s⁻¹

magnitude = 2.0 kg m s⁻¹, directed away from the wall

08

Resolving into two perpendicular components

1.4.3 — represent a vector as two perpendicular components

Addition run backwards. Any vector can be replaced, completely and without loss, by two perpendicular vectors that add to give it. The component along a chosen direction uses the cosine of the angle to that direction; the one at right angles to it uses the sine.

Common error. Reaching for cosine automatically. Neither function belongs to “horizontal” or “vertical” — cosine belongs to the direction the angle is measured from. Check by letting θ → 0: the along-component should become the whole force.

Worked example [4]

A box is pulled along level ground by a rope at 30° to the horizontal with a force of 50 N. Find the horizontal and vertical components.

Fx = 50 cos 30° = 43.3 N (drags the box forward)

Fy = 50 sin 30° = 25.0 N (lifts it, reducing the normal contact force)

Because of that 25.0 N, the normal contact force is less than the weight — which is why friction falls when you pull at an angle rather than pushing down.

09

Weight on a slope

1.4.3 applied — choosing axes that make the problem easy

Nothing says the two perpendicular directions must be horizontal and vertical. On a slope, choose them along the surface and perpendicular to it, and the weight splits into the part that accelerates the object down the slope and the part the surface has to push back.

Tilt the slope

watch the two components trade places as the angle grows
25°
40 N
W sin θ · down slope—
W cos θ · into slope—

Common error. Swapping sine and cosine. Test it at θ = 0°: a flat surface should carry the whole weight (W cos 0° = W) and nothing should slide (W sin 0° = 0). Drag the slider to 0° above and watch it happen.

Worked example [4]

A block of weight 40 N rests on a frictionless slope inclined at 25° to the horizontal. Find the components of its weight along and perpendicular to the slope, and state the normal contact force.

along the slope: 40 sin 25° = 16.9 N

perpendicular: 40 cos 25° = 36.3 N

the surface balances only the perpendicular part, so N = 36.3 N

The 16.9 N is unbalanced, so a frictionless block accelerates down the slope at g sin 25° = 4.1 m s⁻².

10

Equilibrium: the closed triangle

4.2.3 — use a vector triangle to represent coplanar forces in equilibrium

If three coplanar forces hold an object in equilibrium, their resultant is zero — and a resultant of zero means that when you draw them tip to tail, the last arrowhead lands exactly on the first tail. The triangle closes. That is both the test and the method.

  • Equilibrium of a point object: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0.
  • For an extended body you need the second condition too: no resultant torque (4.2.2).
  • The force that balances two others is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to their resultant.
  • Draw the triangle in order, each tail on the previous tip; a triangle drawn out of order will not close.
Worked example [4]

A point is acted on by 3.0 N due east and 4.0 N due north. State the third force that would hold it in equilibrium.

resultant of the two = √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 N at tan⁻¹(4/3) = 53° north of east

the balancing force is equal and opposite:

5.0 N at 53° south of west (bearing 217°)

11

Crossing a river

1.4 applied — perpendicular components are independent

The single most useful consequence of resolving: two perpendicular components do not interfere with each other. A current flowing downstream carries the boat downstream — and does absolutely nothing to how long the crossing takes.

  • Time to cross comes from the component across the river only: t = width / vboat.
  • Drift comes from the current acting for that same time: d = vcurrent × t.
  • The same independence is what makes projectile motion work: horizontal and vertical are solved separately.
Worked example [5]

A boat is steered directly across a river 30 m wide at 3.0 m s⁻¹. The current flows at 2.0 m s⁻¹. Find the resultant velocity, the time to cross and the distance travelled downstream.

v = √(3.0² + 2.0²) = 3.6 m s⁻¹ at tan⁻¹(2.0/3.0) = 34° to the bank-normal

t = 30 / 3.0 = 10 s — the current does not change this

drift = 2.0 × 10 = 20 m downstream
